Reflection

What I learned

Where the course was hardest, what I’ll keep doing, and the moment my thinking changed.

Friction

What matters is writing the rule down so everyone judges the same way.

I found it difficult to make some of the calls on what counts as a failure. A good example was the lawsuit question. Technically the response was right to refuse a summary, because our guidelines say to refuse legal queries. But the user was asking for a summary of a Wall Street Journal article, which a general chat LLM would probably just give them.

I first marked it as a failure, then changed my mind when I came back to it: the answer followed the rules, so the real question was whether the rule itself was right, and that’s one for product to decide. Across the cohort we got different answers on calls like this, which taught me that sometimes there isn’t a single right answer.

Key learnings

What I’ll keep doing

Bring in subject matter experts

Experts from across the business will be key for really specific use cases and anything outside product’s knowledge, both to define what a good answer looks like and to check that our AI judge agrees with them.

Use knowledge graphs to make each step checkable

I finally started to understand how knowledge graphs link facts together, so the AI can follow the trail to an answer and we can check each step it took.

Put my design team on the golden set

They run a lot of usability testing with real users, so their judgement will help make sure the golden set reflects how people actually ask questions, not just how we think they do. They should help define golden-set queries and review the responses.

Aha moment

“There’s no setting where everything is perfect.”

Coming from a design background, I came in thinking AI evals would help us build the perfect agent experience. The big realisation was that it’s really about balancing trade-offs strategically.

Adding guardrails against hallucinations, and building UX trust through sources and citations, both add latency. So it’s the human judgement in those trade-offs that matters when making the call to ship or hold.

For Ascend IQ, that meant choosing accuracy over speed, even with the quarter at stake.
