Module 6, ship-or-hold memo

Should Ascend IQ ship?

Our answer first, then the evidence from five modules behind it.

Decision

Hold.

Hold Ascend IQ’s launch to the top 50 accounts. In our audit it made false claims about named competitors, which fails the Hard release gate we set, and none of the checks that would stop it runs on every release yet.

The arguments

Three reasons to hold

  1. False competitor claims are a legal risk we said we would never ship.

    2 of 20 audited answers made false claims about a named competitor. One reversed our rate-limit comparison with Competitor Z: 1,000 requests a minute against our 500. Our Hard gate allows zero.

  2. Beyond competitors, it states wrong facts that clients would act on.

    2 more of the 20 answers stated false facts: a superseded price ($49 instead of the current $59) and an unconfirmed speaker listed as confirmed. Our Soft gate allows 0% stale prices, and the live price check that would let us ship around it is not built yet.

  3. We can’t yet prove the safeguards work.

    None of our 5 trust metrics has a check running on every release yet, and 2 of 5 (Fairness, Robustness) have no reliable check at all. Our judge caught 2 of 2 false competitor claims, but on only 20 answers with 2 false alarms, and the golden-set result is not measured yet.

Next step

Approve the hold, and ask Sales to stop promising a date

We hold the top-50 launch until four things are done.

  • The competitor-claim judge is live in the pipeline
  • It scores 100% on competitor-comparison answers in the golden set
  • The golden set includes edge-case client questions
  • The live price check is built

Trust metrics and trade-offs

We accept extra seconds to protect trust

Three trust metrics

  • Latency

    Time from question submitted to complete answer shown, tracked as median and 95th percentile against a target set from the logs. Protects “in seconds”: speed is the reason Ascend IQ exists, and slow answers bring back the friction that is driving churn.

  • Hallucination rate
  • UX trust

Low hallucination rateoverlatency

Our users repeat what Ascend IQ tells them in roadmap and leadership decisions. A fast fabricated figure does more damage to a $50K+ relationship than a slower correct one, so we accept extra seconds for grounding checks.

UX trustoverlatency

Showing sources and flagging uncertainty adds time, but it’s what lets users rely on an answer without re-verifying. A fast answer that gets re-checked by hand has saved the user nothing.

Eval spec

What counts as a pass, and who owns it

What counts as a pass
An answer passes if what the user sees contains no false claim about a competitor: either the answer is shown and matches the reference, or it is blocked and replaced by the fallback message.
Only failures the PM confirms count. A false alarm (a correct answer the judge blocked) is logged as a block, not a failure, and used to fix the judge. The PM also tracks how many good answers are blocked each week, so the guard doesn’t make Ascend IQ unhelpful.
Owner
Ascend IQ Product Manager
They review every answer the judge blocks each week, decide whether each block is a true failure or a false alarm (for example rows 3 and 8), and re-check the threshold on a larger test set. Engineering runs the judge in the pipeline, and legal is told about any confirmed false competitor claim.

The gap each product closes first

AI Report Summaries

Robustness

Summaries go straight to clients with no person in between, and the release judge only sees reports in its test set. A new kind of report (long, table-heavy or multi-region) can quietly lose its main finding. The client executive notices first, and the cost is their confidence in research they pay for.

Close it with a drift monitor that alerts when incoming reports differ from the judge’s test set by length, format or sector, and a weekly LLM-judge sample of live summaries against their source reports.

Competitor Signal Tracker

Hallucination rate

The tracker is designed to bring actionable intelligence about competitors to the sales team. Made-up or false data makes it useless, and potentially harmful if the team act on false premises.

Close it with the six-step plan on the next slide.

AI Voice Agent

Fairness

If it understands some callers worse than others, for example by accent or line quality, those callers get wrong billing answers or get passed to a person far more often, and nothing we run today would show the pattern. The cost is billing errors, complaints and lost trust.

Close it with a new eval gate: re-record a subset of the 300 billing questions across accents and line conditions, and fail the release if any group’s pass rate falls more than 5 points below the overall rate. Stratify the supervisor’s weekly 10-call sample by caller group.

Automated Data Tagging

Latency

If tagging slows down or stalls, the models that use these labels keep running on old data and nothing alerts us. Stale data is already a confirmed failure mode: Ascend IQ quoted a superseded price in our audit.

Close it with a drift monitor that alerts when the newest labelled filing is more than 24 hours behind the newest filing received, or when the tagging backlog grows two days in a row. Also watch wrong labels: hallucination rate is only partial, as the approved-list check confirms a label is valid, not that it’s right, and the hand review covers only 50 labels a quarter.

Competitor Signal Tracker

Six steps to close the hallucination gap

  1. Define the failure

    Write down what counts as a hallucination, with a severity tier for each:

    • Invented claim, figure, date or product (critical)
    • Misattributed to the wrong competitor (critical)
    • Rumour or speculation presented as fact (high)
    • Old news presented as new (medium)
    • Score rationale the post doesn’t support (medium)

  2. Build the dataset

    Sample 150 real source posts and outputs from production logs, stratified by source type, competitor and market. Add 30–50 hard cases: thin posts, sarcasm, rumours, conflicting reports, posts naming two competitors. A strategy analyst labels every claim as supported, unsupported or contradicted; a second person labels 20% to measure agreement. Version it and hold part out.

  3. Build the graders

    Deterministic checks: every number, name and date in a summary must appear in the source. An LLM judge splits each summary into atomic claims and checks each against the source. Calibrate it on the labelled set for recall and precision, and don’t trust it until it agrees with the humans at roughly 90% or better.

  4. Metrics and thresholds

    Track the share of claims unsupported and the share of summaries with at least one unsupported claim, severity-weighted. Agree a ship threshold with sales, for example zero critical failures in the golden set.

  5. Cadence

    • Every prompt, model or pipeline change: the full golden set as a release gate
    • Nightly: judge about 25 live outputs and alert if the rate crosses the threshold
    • Weekly: a human reviews the judge’s flagged cases plus 10 random ones, to catch judge drift

  6. Feedback loop

    Add a “this is wrong” button for sales. Every report becomes a new test case, and a monthly error review feeds back into the taxonomy and prompts.

Eval budget

Why a cheaper method is enough for the other three

Fabrication and context misses fail silently and need live scoring. These three don’t.

P1Periodic audit

Overconfident tone

A reviewer samples outputs weekly, oversampling high-stakes ones (financial or operational recommendations), and scores each for confidence versus evidence: unsupported precision, recommendations made on thin evidence, and certainty that outruns the sources. Hedge wording alone doesn’t count as a pass.

Enough because overconfidence is visible to a human reader and drifts gradually, so a risk-weighted weekly sample catches a trend before it spreads. Flagged cases feed back into prompt fixes, and the sample size can grow using the unspent budget.

P2Periodic audit

Data bias

Each week, reviewers compare matched US and APAC scenarios and score accuracy, omissions, unsupported assumptions and recommendation quality, not just summary length or detail. Samples are stratified by client type and data characteristics.

Enough because bias shows up as a pattern across many outputs, so a stratified matched-pair sample detects it without scoring every response live. At P2, a weekly review is proportionate, and it can move to a higher frequency if a skew appears.

P3Code-based check

Slow answers

An automated release test runs a fixed set of long multi-step questions and fails the release if any exceed 2.0 seconds. A production alert on p95 latency, built on existing telemetry, covers live traffic between releases.

Enough because latency is directly measurable by rule, so it needs no judgement or LLM scoring. The release gate catches regressions, and the p95 alert catches load spikes and slow paths in production. At P3, this is proportionate.

Reflection

What I learned

Where the course was hardest, what I’ll keep doing, and the moment my thinking changed.

Friction

What matters is writing the rule down so everyone judges the same way.

I found it difficult to make some of the calls on what counts as a failure. A good example was the lawsuit question. Technically the response was right to refuse a summary, because our guidelines say to refuse legal queries. But the user was asking for a summary of a Wall Street Journal article, which a general chat LLM would probably just give them.

I first marked it as a failure, then changed my mind when I came back to it: the answer followed the rules, so the real question was whether the rule itself was right, and that’s one for product to decide. Across the cohort we got different answers on calls like this, which taught me that sometimes there isn’t a single right answer.

Key learnings

What I’ll keep doing

Bring in subject matter experts

Experts from across the business will be key for really specific use cases and anything outside product’s knowledge, both to define what a good answer looks like and to check that our AI judge agrees with them.

Use knowledge graphs to make each step checkable

I finally started to understand how knowledge graphs link facts together, so the AI can follow the trail to an answer and we can check each step it took.

Put my design team on the golden set

They run a lot of usability testing with real users, so their judgement will help make sure the golden set reflects how people actually ask questions, not just how we think they do. They should help define golden-set queries and review the responses.

Aha moment

“There’s no setting where everything is perfect.”

Coming from a design background, I came in thinking AI evals would help us build the perfect agent experience. The big realisation was that it’s really about balancing trade-offs strategically.

Adding guardrails against hallucinations, and building UX trust through sources and citations, both add latency. So it’s the human judgement in those trade-offs that matters when making the call to ship or hold.

For Ascend IQ, that meant choosing accuracy over speed, even with the quarter at stake.
