The decision

Should Ascend IQ ship?

Decision: HOLD. Hold Ascend IQ's launch to the top 50 accounts: in our audit it made false claims about named competitors, which fails the Hard release gate we set, and none of the checks that would stop it runs on every release yet.

Module 6
Ship-or-hold memo
The arguments

1. False competitor claims are a legal risk we said we would never ship. Evidence: 2 of 20 audited answers made false claims about a named competitor (one reversed our rate-limit comparison with Competitor Z: 1,000 req/min vs our 500). Our Hard gate allows zero.
2. Beyond competitors, it states wrong facts that clients would act on. Evidence: 2 more of the 20 answers stated false facts: a superseded price ($49 instead of the current $59) and an unconfirmed speaker listed as confirmed. Our Soft gate allows 0% stale prices, and the live price check that would let us ship around it is not built yet.
3. We can't yet prove the safeguards work. Evidence: None of our 5 trust metrics has a check running on every release yet, and 2 of 5 (Fairness, Robustness) have no reliable check at all. Our judge caught 2 of 2 false competitor claims, but on only 20 answers with 2 false alarms, and the golden-set result is not measured yet.

Next step

Approve holding the top-50 launch, and ask Sales to stop promising a date, until four things are done: the competitor-claim judge is live in the pipeline, it scores 100% on competitor-comparison answers in the golden set, the golden set includes edge-case client questions, and the live price check is built. Please confirm by Friday 27 November 2026. I'll bring the golden-set results to the first release review after the golden set has run, with a ship-or-hold call.

Insights

What I learned

Where the course was hardest, what you’ll keep doing, and the moment your thinking changed.

Friction

I found it difficult to make some of the calls on what counts as a failure. A good example was the lawsuit question. Technically the response was right to refuse a summary, because our guidelines say to refuse legal queries. But the user was asking for a summary of a Wall Street Journal article, which a general chat LLM would probably just give them. I first marked it as a failure, then changed my mind when I came back to it: the answer followed the rules, so the real question was whether the rule itself was right, and that's one for product to decide. Across the cohort we got different answers on calls like this, which taught me that sometimes there isn't a single right answer. What matters is writing the rule down so everyone judges the same way.

Key learnings

Bringing in subject matter experts from across the business will be key for really specific use cases and anything outside product's knowledge, both to define what a good answer looks like and to check that our AI judge agrees with them. I also finally started to understand how knowledge graphs link facts together, so the AI can follow the trail to an answer and we can check each step it took. From a design perspective, I need to make sure my team are part of defining golden set queries and reviewing the responses as well. They run a lot of usability testing with real users, so their judgement will help make sure the golden set reflects how people actually ask questions, not just how we think they do.

Aha moment

Coming from a design background, I came in thinking AI evals would help us build the perfect agent experience. The big realisation was that it's really about balancing trade-offs strategically. For example, adding guardrails against hallucinations, and building UX trust through sources and citations, both add latency. There's no setting where everything is perfect, so it's the human judgement in those trade-offs that matters when making the call to ship or hold. For Ascend IQ, that meant choosing accuracy over speed, even with the quarter at stake.
